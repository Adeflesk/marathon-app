# Train easy, extend the specific, protect the ankle

The training practices with the best evidence for a recreational runner chasing 3:59 are: enough weekly volume, with most of it genuinely easy; one controlled quality day that moves from general speed early on to threshold, then marathon-specific running; a few long runs of 30–32 km about 3–5 weeks out, with marathon-pace (MP) finishes building every other week; cutbacks every 3–4 weeks; and a strict taper that drops volume every week but keeps some intensity. Your plan already gets most of the skeleton right. The 25 → 55 km build in 3 km steps, the 4th-week cutbacks, the 3-week falling taper, the late MP finishes and the Tue/Thu strength all match coach and research consensus. It diverges in four places. The long run is **58% of the week at peak**, against 25–33% in Hansons and Daniels. Three back-to-back 30–32 km runs each carry a 12 km MP finish. The Wednesday session is the same continuous tempo every week and vanishes in cutback and taper weeks. And a **55 km peak is at the bottom of what sub-4 runners actually do**, so there is little margin. For an AI weekly coach, the evidence supports making weeks harder only when several signals agree over 2–3 weeks, changing one thing at a time, and letting ankle pain veto any push. Most of the specific push/hold/ease thresholds below are reasoned from the sources rather than tested, and they are labelled that way. Community input is thin: no Reddit, LetsRun or Fetcheveryone threads could be retrieved, so "what runners report" rests on one Runner's World case study and a handful of other forum posts.

**How to read this outline.** Paces assume your app's settings: MP 5:41/km, tempo 5:25/km, long 6:30/km, easy 6:45/km. Week numbers are your app's plan weeks (week 1 starts Mon 19 Oct 2026; week 21 is race week). Statements tagged **[Sources]** come from cited material. Statements tagged **[Inferred]** are rules reasoned from that material for your situation and have not been tested as written.

## 1. Volume decides the most, and 55 km sits right at the edge

**[Sources]** Volume is the training variable most strongly tied to marathon time. A Strava study of 151,813 marathons found finish time strongly linked to weekly volume and to the share of that volume run easy. Mean volume was 45 km/week, and the fastest runners ran more than three times what the slowest ran ([Muniz-Pumares et al. 2024, AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/54cba16f-a942-47e5-9e58-05f63c89cf9f/full)). A summary of Strava data puts **3:30–4:00 finishers at 50–60 km/week on 5–6 days, and 4:00+ finishers at 35–45 km on 3–4 days** ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/sub-3-marathon-training/)). The named plans peak higher: Higdon Intermediate 1 at 44 mi (about 71 km) on 5 days ([Hal Higdon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/intermediate-1-marathon/)), and the Hansons Beginner plan around 55–57 mi (about 90 km) on 6 days ([Luke Humphrey](https://lukehumphreyrunning.com/the-classic-hanson-marathon-training-programs/)). Commercial 4-day sub-4 plans peak lower, at about 37.5 mi (60 km) ([Final Surge](https://www.finalsurge.com/coach/runyourpersonalbest/plan/104233)). The one detailed Runner's World sub-4 case study went from 4:33 to 3:49 on 4 runs a week plus cycling, peaking at **about 65 km** ([Runner's World](https://www.runnersworld.co.za/training/i-broke-4-hours-in-the-marathon-thanks-to-these-training-tweaks/)). A club guide sets readiness for a sub-4 build at a half marathon under about 1:55 and 35–40+ km/week held for 2–3 months ([Coach Parry](https://blog.coachparry.com/?p=1522)).

**[Sources]** The 10% rule doesn't hold up as a hard limit. In a 532-runner trial, a 10%-per-week progression produced the same injury rate as a much faster one (20.8% vs 20.3%) ([Buist et al. 2008](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/89x92/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-related-injuries-in-novice-runners-a-randomized-controlled-trial)). Novices who raised distance by **more than 30%** over two weeks trended toward more injuries ([Nielsen et al. 2014](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn)). The clearest running-specific risk signal in a 2025 study was a **single run more than 10% longer than the longest run in the previous 30 days**. Week-to-week volume ratios showed no relationship with injury ([Frandsen et al., BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/3/146); figures from the abstract summary, full text not accessed).

**Where your plan stands.** The 3 km/week steps (at most about 12% per week) are comfortably inside the evidence. The peak of 55 km is defensible given the ankle, but it is the floor of the sub-4 band rather than the middle. **[Inferred]** If the build goes well, letting the coach lift peak weeks to about 58–62 km by lengthening the Monday and Saturday runs would add margin without changing the 4-day shape. That figure is the low end of the Runner's World and Higdon range. Two spots in the generated plan trip the sourced guards:

| Plan spot | What happens | Guard it trips |
|---|---|---|
| Wk 15 → 16 | Weekly km jumps 42 → 55 (+31%) coming out of a cutback | Near the >30% flag, though only +6% on the pre-cutback week (52 km) |
| Wk 7 → 8 | Long run 16 → 18 km (+12.5%) | >10% over the longest run in 30 days |
| Wk 15 → 16 | Long run 26 (wk 14) → 30 km (+15%) | >10% over the longest run in 30 days |

## 2. Easy days must actually be easy, but not so slow your stride breaks down

**[Sources]** Faster marathoners run a bigger share of their volume easy. Slower recreational runners often run only 50–60% easy, with "inverted" distributions tied to slower times ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/sub-3-marathon-training/); [London Met](https://repository.londonmet.ac.uk/9883/)). Polarized and pyramidal intensity patterns showed no clear winner in recreational athletes ([JSSM](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-18-708.xml-abst)). Most successful amateurs train pyramidally: lots of easy running, some moderate, a little hard. Coaches agree that **the most common mistake is easy runs drifting too fast** ([TrainingPeaks](https://trainingpeaks.com/blog/how-to-break-the-4-hour-marathon-barrier)). Higdon puts easy and long runs **30–90 s/mi (about 20–55 s/km) slower than MP** ([Hal Higdon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/intermediate-1-marathon/)). Hansons' Luke Humphrey warns 4-hour marathoners against forcing very slow running. It can produce a shuffling stride and an unexpectedly high heart rate, and everyday pace for this group is "pretty close" to race pace ([Luke Humphrey](https://lukehumphreyrunning.com/easy-paces-and-the-4-hour-marathoner/)). The Runner's World runner credited **running long runs by conversational effort, not pace**, as a key change ([Runner's World](https://www.runnersworld.co.za/training/i-broke-4-hours-in-the-marathon-thanks-to-these-training-tweaks/)).

**Where your plan stands.** With one quality day plus some MP on Sundays, the plan is naturally about 75–85% easy, which is pyramidal and fine. Your easy pace of 6:45/km is MP + 64 s/km, a bit slower than Higdon's band. That is acceptable as a ceiling, but don't use it as a target. **[Inferred]** The coach should judge easy runs by effort and heart-rate trend, not by hitting 6:45. It should flag easy runs that average **faster than about 6:00/km** (MP + 20 s) as "too fast". It shouldn't nag about runs between 6:00 and 6:45 if heart rate is steady.

## 3. Long runs: a few big ones, MP finishes every other week

**[Sources]** The camps genuinely disagree on long runs:

- **Hansons and Daniels** cap the long run at about 16 mi, about 2.5–3 h, and **25–33% of weekly volume**. They criticise plans where the long run is half the week ([Luke Humphrey](https://lukehumphreyrunning.com/hansons-marathon-method-the-16-miler/); [Canadian Running](https://runningmagazine.ca/?p=99607)).
- **Higdon** uses two 20-milers on a 44 mi peak, so long runs reach about 45% of the week ([Hal Higdon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/intermediate-1-marathon/)).
- **McMillan** sets the longest run by time. For a 4:00 marathoner it is 3:30–4:30, done **3–5 weeks out and not repeated every weekend**, with injury-prone runners staying at the short end ([McMillan Running](https://www.mcmillanrunning.com/?p=141438)).
- **Strava data** show peak long runs of **29–32 km** across nearly all ability groups. What differs is the volume around them ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/sub-3-marathon-training/)).

On MP work, the recommended sequence is: long runs fully easy at first, then easy into moderate in the second half, and MP finishes only later ([Luke Humphrey](https://lukehumphreyrunning.com/long-run-pace-progression/)). An 8 km MP finish in a 28 km run is "not meant for beginners" and **not weekly even for veterans** ([Canadian Running](https://runningmagazine.ca/?p=80783)). Higdon allows a fast finish no more than once every three weekends ([Hal Higdon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/intermediate-1-marathon/)). Pfitzinger starts MP runs by effort and dials in race pace by the third or fourth one ([Running With Rock](https://runningwithrock.com/pfitz-marathon-training-explained/)). Canova-style plans run 30–35 km with the last 10 km at MP, but the sample sources for those are unreliable ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/special-block-training/)). Practise race fuelling on every run of 20 km or more. The Runner's World case study used half a gel every 5 km ([Runner's World](https://www.runnersworld.co.za/training/i-broke-4-hours-in-the-marathon-thanks-to-these-training-tweaks/)).

**Where your plan stands.**

- **Matches:** MP finishes start late (week 12), after a long easy-only phase. The 32 km cap lands 3–4 weeks out. A 32 km run at 6:30/km takes about 3:28, inside McMillan's window.
- **Diverges:**
  - The peak long run is **58% of a 55 km week**, about double the Hansons/Daniels ceiling. On 4 days that is largely unavoidable unless the weekday runs grow.
  - Weeks 16–18 stack **30, 32 and 32 km on consecutive Sundays, each with a 12 km MP finish**. Every source above says don't repeat the biggest run, or a hard MP finish, every week.
  - MP finishes appear **every** build and peak week from week 12 (4 → 6 → 8 → 12 km). The sources point to every other week.

**[Inferred] Long-run rules for the coach:**

- **Total 30–32 km runs:** 2 at most, in weeks 16 and 18. Week 17 drops to about 26–28 km, all easy, or with a short steady finish.
- **MP finishes:** alternate MP weeks with easy weeks from week 12. Grow the MP portion 6 → 8 → 10 → 12 km across the MP weeks.
- **Time cap:** never more than about 3:30 h, whatever the distance.
- **Spike guard:** the long run is never more than 1.1 × the longest run actually completed in the last 30 days.
- **Ankle:** no MP finish while ankle pain is 3 or more. The first long runs after the sprain are flat and on non-cambered paths.

## 4. Speed work: go from general to specific, with near-MP threshold work as the core

**[Sources]** For one quality day a week, the most useful session types are:

- **Threshold and sub-threshold work** between MP and half-marathon pace. This includes Hansons "strength" at MP − 10 s/mile, reps of 1–3 mi with short jogs ([Luke Humphrey](https://lukehumphreyrunning.com/marathon-strength-workouts/)); Daniels cruise intervals at T pace with about a 5:1 work:rest ratio ([Goodreads notes on Daniels](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/20762026-daniels-running-formula/77147819-adrian-gonzalez?page=3)); and Norwegian-singles reps such as 5 × 6 min or 3 × 10 min with 60 s jogs ([Marathon Handbook](https://marathonhandbook.com/norwegian-singles-training/)).
- **MP running**.
- **VO2max intervals** of 600–1200 m at about 5 km pace ([Running With Rock](https://runningwithrock.com/pfitz-marathon-training-explained/)).

Hills and strides are low-volume add-ons. Coaches converge on moving from general to specific: strides and hills in the base, speed or threshold work early, sub-threshold work in the middle, and **MP-specific work in the last 6–8 weeks** (Canova's 95–105% of MP) ([RunnersConnect on Canova](https://runnersconnect.net/special-block-training/)). Pfitzinger is the exception: he puts VO2 work late, as a sharpener ([Running With Rock](https://runningwithrock.com/pfitz-marathon-training-explained/)). Hansons does the opposite: early speed (for example 12 × 400 m), then strength work later ([r/AdvancedRunning summary](https://lr.us.psf.lt/r/AdvancedRunning/comments/4twcbg/the_summer_series_hansons)). Jeff Gaudette says VO2 gains make MP feel easier but don't improve fuel efficiency, so he prioritises marathon-specific work ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/yasso-800s-marathon-time-predict/)). **Yasso 800s have no physiological basis as a predictor** (same source).

Progress mainly by **extending time at a fixed pace**: more reps, then longer reps, then shorter recoveries. Raise pace only after a race or time trial. Pfitzinger and Hansons both warn against running faster than prescribed ([Luke Humphrey](https://lukehumphreyrunning.com/marathon-strength-workouts/); [RunnersConnect on Canova](https://runnersconnect.net/special-block-training/)). In a cohort of Boston runners, each extra weekly quality session went with a faster finish, but that is correlational ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/sub-3-marathon-training/)). One quality day is viable.

**Where your plan stands.** The tempo from week 8 fits the order the sources recommend. But the Wednesday is a single continuous tempo every week (12 → 32 min at 5:25). It has no variety and no MP-specific phase, and it **is dropped entirely in cutback and taper weeks**. **[Inferred]** On cutback weeks, keep the session and cut the reps by 30–40%. Swap the continuous tempo for the menu below.

## 5. Cutbacks and taper: your structure already matches the best data

**[Sources]** Coaches use a down week every 3–4 weeks. Older runners, or those with demanding jobs or families, go every 3rd week. Volume drops by roughly **20–30%**, and the long run takes much of the cut ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/why-planned-down-weeks-are-important/); [Laura Norris](https://lauranorrisrunning.com/how-to-effectively-use-cutback-weeks-in-your-training/)). No controlled evidence exists on the best depth or frequency. For tapers, meta-analyses favour **cutting volume by 41–60% while keeping intensity and frequency** ([Wang et al. 2023](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Effects-of-tapering-on-performance-in-endurance-athletes.pdf)). In 158,117 recreational Strava marathoners, a **strict 3-week taper**, with volume falling every week, was linked to a **median 5 min 32 s (2.6%) faster finish** than a relaxed 1-week taper. A strict 2-week taper came close behind ([Smyth & Lawlor 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8506252)). Higdon's last 20-miler sits 3 weeks out ([Hal Higdon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/intermediate-1-marathon/)).

**Where your plan stands.**

- **Matches:**
  - Cutbacks are −20% on volume and −25% on the long run.
  - The taper falls 55 → 41 → 30 → about 14 km plus the race. That is a strict 3-week taper, the best-performing pattern in the Strava data.
  - The last 32 km run is 3 weeks out.
- **Diverges:** taper weeks 19–20 have **no quality at all**. The evidence says keep a short MP or threshold touch, as in the menu above. Race week is about −75% before the race, deeper than the 41–60% research band but similar to Higdon. Leave it.

**[Inferred]**

- **Taper:** the coach must never flatten or bump the taper. A taper that rises or holds counts as "relaxed" and loses most of the benefit.
- **Cutback weeks:** the coach may not push. It may pull a cutback a week earlier (a 3-week cycle) if the ease-off rules fire two weeks running.

## 6. Strength twice a week pays off, and the ankle block should never stop

**[Sources]** Strength training added to running improves running economy by **about 2–8%** in most trials ([Blagrove et al. 2018](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Effects_of_strength_training_on_the_physiological_determinants_of_middle-_and_long-distance_running_performance_a_systematic_review/9619553)). Heavy lifting has a small, significant effect. **Plyometrics help significantly at speeds of 12 km/h or below**, which covers your 10.6 km/h race pace. Combined programmes show the largest, though least certain, effect. Trials ran 1–4 sessions a week, and frequency didn't change the size of the gain ([Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11052887/)). Across sport generally, strength training cut injuries to about a third (**RR 0.32**), while stretching did nothing ([Lauersen et al. 2014, BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871)). Those trials are mostly team sports, not runners. For the taper, sources say drop the heaviest lifts about 2–3 weeks out but keep **two light 20–30 min sessions a week** ([RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/running-questions/when-should-i-stop-strength-training-at-the-gym-before-the-marathon/); [Project Physio](https://projectphysio.co.nz/?p=826)). For the ankle, structured exercise cuts the odds of re-spraining by **43%**, and by **52% once total training passes about 900 minutes** ([Bleakley, BMJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5112177)). An unsupervised 8-week balance programme cut recurrences by about 35% ([Hupperets et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6044595/table/T3)). Bracing has strong evidence for preventing recurrence ([Doherty et al., BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/51/2/113)).

**Where your plan stands.** Tuesday Strength B (upper body and trunk) and Thursday Strength A (single-leg, calves, glutes, ankle) match the supported two-session dose, and the app already lightens strength near tempo runs and in cutback and taper weeks. No source sets the rules for exactly where strength goes relative to the quality day. The Thursday-after-Wednesday "hard then easy" layout is reasonable but unvalidated. **[Inferred]**

- **Ankle block:** keep balance, single-leg calf raises and banded eversion/inversion work running all the way through race week at a low dose. It costs little fatigue, and the 900-minute benefit takes about 9–12 weeks of 10–15 min/day to reach.
- **Heavy lifts:** drop them from week 19.
- **Plyometrics:** add low-level work (pogo hops, skipping) only once hopping on the injured side is pain-free.

## 7. Coming back from the ankle sprain

> General information only, not medical advice. A physio or doctor should set return-to-run criteria for your specific sprain, including its grade, and rule out a fracture or tendon injury.

**[Sources]**

- **Healing time:** ligaments take **at least 6 weeks to 3 months** to heal ([Livestrong](https://livestrong.com/article/425025-when-can-i-restart-running-after-an-ankle-sprain)). More than 40% of patients don't fully recover, because of persistent pain, re-injury or instability ([Bleakley, BMJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5112177)).
- **Surfaces:** return on predictable ground (flat road, track, treadmill) and avoid trails until strength and balance return ([Runner's World SA](https://www.runnersworld.co.za/injuries/how-to-return-to-running-after-spraining-your-ankle/); [Trail Runner](https://www.trailrunnermag.com/training/injuries-and-treatment-training/bounce-back-faster-after-an-ankle-sprain/?scope=anon)).
- **Order:** build duration first, then intensity ([Brooks](https://www.brooksrunning.com/en_ie/blog/advice-tips/sprained-ankle-101.html)).
- **Self-tests:** clinic readiness markers include **25–30 single-leg calf raises**, **30 s single-leg balance with eyes closed**, and hop tests at 90% or more of the other side. They are useful but not validated thresholds ([Exploration of Medicine](https://www.explorationpub.com/Journals/emd/Article/100736); [RxDx](https://www.rxdx.co.in/pediatrics/articles/return_to_sport_criteria_after_ankle_sprain)).
- **Pain rules:** the best-tested pain-monitoring model allows pain up to about 5/10 during exercise, provided it settles by the next morning and doesn't rise week to week. It was developed for Achilles tendinopathy, not ankle sprains ([Silbernagel et al. 2007 summary](https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/blog/continued-sports-activity-using-a-pain-monitoring-model-during-rehabilitation-in-patients-with-achilles-tendinopathy/)).
- **When to stop:** stop and get it checked if the ankle starts hurting again. A suspected sprain can be a fracture or a torn tendon ([AOFAS](https://www.aofas.org/news/news-articles/2021/08/19/ankle-sprains-while-running-symptoms-and-treatment)).

No source compared hills with track intervals after a sprain, or addressed camber or downhills directly.

**Where your plan stands.** Three easy-only weeks at 25 km, and tempo held back until week 8, fit this guidance well. **[Inferred] Order for bringing intensity back**, gated on pain ≤ 2 and stable:

1. Flat strides.
2. Fartlek and threshold on flat road or treadmill.
3. Smooth 4–6% uphills, walking down on even pavement.
4. Track VO2 reps, run on the straights or outer lanes, only after a few pain-free threshold weeks.

Avoid downhill repeats, cambered roads and trails throughout the build. Barcelona is flat, so hills are optional for race specificity. Cycling is a pain-free way to hold fitness if running has to pause ([Runner's World UK forum](https://forums.runnersworld.co.uk/discussion/346120/sprained-ankle-during-half-marathon-training)).

## 8. Decision rules for the AI weekly coach

### What the sources say about adapting
**[Sources]**

- **Commercial apps adapt mostly through paces.** Runna changes targets only when a runner *consistently* beats or misses them, ignores reps that look wrong, recommends pace shifts worth only 20–30 s of predicted race time at a time, and doesn't ask how sessions felt ([Runna support](https://support.runna.com/en/articles/14656203-what-are-pace-insights-and-how-do-they-work); [the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/09/28/is-runna-ai/)). Physical therapists have linked Runna to injuries from aggressive pacing (same source).
- **Garmin Coach goes both ways.** It makes workouts harder when benchmark runs are faster, or HR lower, than expected, and easier after missed workouts ([shoulditrain review](https://www.shoulditrain.com/blog/garmin-coach-review)).
- **Readiness-gated hard days.** In an HRV-guided trial, recreational runners who did hard sessions only on "ready" days did *fewer* hard sessions and improved more ([Vesterinen 2016](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/50625)).
- **Missed time (Pfitzinger).** With fewer than 10 days missed, resume where the schedule says. If 10 or more days are lost in the final 8 weeks, be open to revising the goal ([Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/what-to-do-when-your-marathon-training-gets-interrupted)).
- **Returning from illness.** One coach suggests about 50%, then about 70%, then full volume, with no speedwork at first ([The Mother Runners](https://www.themotherrunners.com/what-to-do-if-you-get-sick-during-marathon-training/)).
- **Load ratios.** Acute:chronic load ratios (ACWR) are contested, and one critique concludes the framework should be dropped ([Impellizzeri 2021](https://iris.univr.it/retrieve/e34cfb98-e922-4c2f-aab5-18583ab7e31b/Impellizzeri_What%20Role%20Do%20Chronic%20Workloads%20Play_SportMed_2021.pdf)). Use them as a guardrail at most.
- **Aerobic decoupling.** Drift in pace:HR within a run of under 5% signals good aerobic endurance ([TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)), but it needs split data that average-only runs don't have.

### What your coach can and can't see [Inferred]
Smashrun gives one average pace and HR per run. So the coach **can't confirm rep paces**, only whether the whole run's average matches what the planned session should average. It should compare against that expected average and ask in the check-in whether the reps were on target. The best available fitness trend is an **easy-run efficiency score**: speed ÷ average HR on runs that were planned easy and stayed in the easy pace band, using a 3-week rolling median against the 3 weeks before. Heat raises HR, so a single hot week shouldn't count as decline.

### Push / hold / ease off [Inferred: reasoned from the sources above, with untested thresholds that need tuning]

**Step 1 — Ankle override, checked first, always.**

| Ankle signal | Action |
|---|---|
| Pain 0–2, stable or falling | Pushing is allowed if step 2 also allows it |
| Pain 3–4, **or** up 2+ points on last week | **Hold**: no km increase; drop the MP finish; if the note links pain to speed or hills, make Wednesday steady or easy on the flat |
| Pain ≥ 5, **or** the note mentions next-morning pain, limping, swelling or giving way | **Ease**: about −20–30% km (the app's 0.8 factor); all runs easy; long run shortened; suggest seeing a physio |
| Pain ≥ 5 two weeks running | Stop progression; strongly recommend a professional assessment before any quality work |

If pain rises with *speed or surfaces*, cut intensity first. If it rises with *duration or the long run*, cut the long run first. The free-text note is how the coach tells the two apart. Adding a "worse the morning after? y/n" question to the check-in would make the next-morning rule usable.

**Step 2 — Readiness gate for a harder week.** All of these must hold:

- Feel is 4 or 5 in each of the last 2 weeks.
- All 4 runs were completed in each of the last 2 weeks.
- At least 2 of the last 3 quality sessions were on target, by whole-run average and by the note.
- Easy runs were not "too fast" (not averaging under about 6:00/km).
- The easy-run efficiency score is stable or rising.
- Next week is not a cutback, taper or race week.

**Step 3 — If pushing, pull one lever only.** Never change pace and volume in the same week.

| Lever | Step size | Hard caps |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly km | +3–5 km (≤ +10%) over the plan week | No more than 1.1 × the average of the last 3 *completed* weeks; peak ceiling of about 60–62 km |
| Wednesday session | +1 rep, or about +5 min of work at the same pace | Threshold work ≤ about 10% of weekly km; recoveries never shorter than the menu |
| Long-run MP finish | +2 km at MP | Only in an MP week; MP portion ≤ 12–14 km |
| Long-run distance | +2 km | ≤ 32 km, ≤ about 3:30 h, ≤ 1.1 × the longest run in the last 30 days, at most two runs of 30–32 km |
| Paces | About 1–2 s/km faster | Only after 2–3 consistent sessions; bigger shifts only from a 10 km time trial or tune-up race |

**Step 4 — Hold** when feel is 3, or 1 run was missed, or one key session missed its target, or the efficiency score dipped by a few percent. A hold keeps the planned week as it stands, with no make-up runs.

**Step 5 — Ease off** when feel is 1–2, or the efficiency score falls together with low feel, or the runner reports illness, or 2+ runs were missed.

- **Short absences:** about −10–20% km, and Wednesday becomes easy with strides. After roughly a week off, resume the scheduled week at about 70–80% volume with no quality.
- **2+ weeks off or illness:** about 50%, then 70%, then full volume, with quality returning in the second week.
- **10 or more days lost in weeks 14–21:** slow MP and tempo by a few s/km and tell the runner the 3:59 goal may need revising.
- **Missed long run:** never double up. The next long run is capped by the 1.1 × spike guard.

**Step 6 — Fixed shape.** The coach may move numbers inside the shape, but never moves a cutback later, never cancels one, never raises a taper week, and never schedules anything on or after race day. It explains every change in one plain sentence, because users distrust adaptations they can't see the reason for ([the5krunner on Garmin](https://the5krunner.com/2024/11/12/garmin-adaptive-plans-get-improved-explanations/)).

**Checkpoints.** A 10 km time trial or tune-up race in a cutback week (week 11 or 15) is the sound way to re-derive paces and test whether 3:59 is realistic. A half marathon under about 1:55 is the readiness marker one coach uses ([Coach Parry](https://blog.coachparry.com/?p=1522)).

## Conclusion

The research points to one main change for your coach: its main way to make weeks harder should be **making the Wednesday session and the MP finishes more marathon-specific, not making Sundays longer**. Your Sundays are already at or past the evidence ceiling as a share of the week. Volume headroom sits mostly in the Monday and Saturday runs, up to about 60 km, and in giving cutback and taper weeks a shorter version of the quality session instead of none. The three-week peak block is where the plan most needs softening. Alternating a big run with a moderate one makes the 32 km cap safer for the ankle and leaves MP sessions fresher.

The weakest link in the evidence is the part your coach depends on most: no published, validated rule says "this pace-and-HR pattern means push by this much". The thresholds in section 8 are reasoned from the sources rather than proven, so log each decision and how the following week went, and tune them over the build. The community picture is also thin. The searches retrieved no Reddit, LetsRun or Fetcheveryone threads, so "what runners say works" here rests mainly on one Runner's World case study. It agrees with the coaches: more consistent easy volume, long runs by effort, one structured quality day, practised fuelling, and a slightly conservative first half at Barcelona.
